## 2. Hobbs on clarity

### 2.1 The distinctions (Magee's, as Hobbs reports, tests and extends them)

1. **Presentational clarity vs discursive clarity** (ms p.3). *Presentational* (also "content structure") = clarity of the ideas and of the structure of the argument as a whole; Magee: one must think the matter "through to the bottom" to "a complete grasp of its presentational structure" (Magee 454, quoted ms p.3). *Discursive* = clarity of the language the ideas are put in. Magee ranks presentational as primary.
2. **Discursive clarity splits into overall text structure (secondary) and sentence-level clarity (tertiary)** (ms p.3-4). Magee's complaint is that sentence-level clarity is what gets praised, yet it is the least important of the three.
3. **Clarity of thought vs clarity of writing are independent** (ms p.3-4, 6). Kant: poor sentence-level clarity, excellent presentational clarity. Russell: clear at all levels. Wittgenstein (in Magee's view): clear sentences, unclear presentation. Hobbs's corpus numbers support the first two readings and she leaves Wittgenstein's presentational clarity to the reader. Conclusion she upholds: a writer "does not need to conform to discursive clarity at the sentence-level in order to be considered a great philosopher" (ms p.6).
4. **Joll's "default clarity"** (Nicholas Joll, *Telos* 2009; ms p.4): four desirable elements, only one of which, *rigor* (hypotaxis plus a logically valid argument), goes beyond the sentence; the others are explication of terms and avoidance of jargon and verbosity. Joll does not rank them. Other sentence-level features the literature names: avoid long words where short ones will do; use personal pronouns to separate others' arguments from one's own.
5. **Surface proxies of sentence-level clarity, and their limits** (ms p.4-6, Table 1). Hobbs measures self-mention per 1,000 words, average words per sentence (WPS), mean word length, and standardised type-token ratio (STTR, lexical variety). A "discursively clear" philosophy text should show high self-mention, low WPS, short words, low STTR. Results: 70 recent analytic-philosophy articles 10.14 / 30.04 / 5.0 / 32.8; Russell 9.76 / 24.37 / 4.65 / 40.48; Wittgenstein 29.63 / 17 / 4.19 / 32.81; Kant 6.84 / 35.5 / 4.86 / 33.4. Three proxies behave as expected; **STTR does not: Russell, the clearest writer, has the widest vocabulary.** So lexical variety is not a clarity defect, and a tool must not treat it as one.
6. **Clarity is relative to a discourse community** (ms p.2-3, note 7). "What is considered clear language in one discourse community is often far from acceptable or clear in another": organisational-behaviour experts identify abstract language and nominalisation with clarity and precision, the opposite of the philosophers' norm. Familiarity with a text type "determines, to a large degree, how clear it is to the reader". New members of a discipline must follow its conventions more strictly. Clarity is therefore a relation between a text and a reader with particular expectations, not an intrinsic property of sentences. Hobbs restricts her own analysis to "clarity as defined within philosophy" (note 7).
7. **Writing as transcription vs writing as inquiry** (ms p.6-8). Magee's points 2 and 3 (grasp the whole structure before writing; do it in isolation) rest on "a faulty view of writing, one based on the idea that you must first know what you are going to say before you write". Against this: van den Bergh & Rijlaarsdam (the probability of generating ideas increases during writing), Berthoff, O'Leary, Bean's "brouillon stage" (a journey into disorder out of which an essay is forged), Elbow ("grow and cook a message"), Fulwiler (words give concrete form to thought). "The more one writes, the more his ideas develop" (ms p.7).
8. **Mozartians vs Beethovians** (Kellogg 2006, cited ms p.7-8). Mozartians plan linearly: a long period of reading and thinking, then writing. Beethovians work recursively, "using the page as an external extension of their mind, where they can list ideas, move them around, delete and add to them". For both, writing down gives the mind "a visualization, a physical shape to its ideas", which lets the thinker "see new paths or holes in the existing path" (ms p.8). Magee idealises the Mozartian; most people, and most lives, do not permit it.
9. **The isolated thinker vs collaborative thinking** (ms p.8-9). The blackboard seminar: a single thought unpacked, rephrased, defended, challenged by a group, with the presentational structure reached only at the end. Magee's "close attention to others" is limited to the writer's private model of the reader and to transmitting an already-formed message; it overlooks the reader's direct involvement in forming the ideas (the -etic vs -emic contrast). The isolated thinker "cannot understand his audience and get their much needed feedback unless he comes out of isolation".
10. **Thought and language as separate** (ms p.9-10). Hobbs links the "pure thought first, words after" view to a positivist tradition in which language is "an afterthought to cognition", and to Rorty's report of a philosophical view of writing as "an unfortunate necessity". Her final position: "putting thoughts to words can itself be a form of inquiry, whatever the discipline, whilst acknowledging that the particular choice of words is less important than the ideas themselves" (ms p.10).

### 2.2 When is unclarity legitimate, and what is a fair demand for clarity?

- Hobbs does not defend obscurity as such; her claim is about priority. Sentence-level unclarity does not disqualify a text whose ideas and structure are clear (Kant). Sentence polish is tertiary; demanding it first inverts the order.
- A demand for clarity is fair when it is made at the right level and against the right community's norms: (i) are the ideas and the shape of the argument thought through (presentational)? (ii) is the text organised so a reader can follow that shape (text structure)? (iii) only then, are sentences lucid by the standards of the readers this text is for?
- Unfair demands: applying one discipline's surface norms (short sentences, pronouns, no nominalisation) to another; treating lexical variety or long words as unclarity per se; treating technical terms as jargon when the audience is fluent in them.
- Hobbs also, implicitly, makes a fairness claim about *process*: it is unreasonable to require the writer to be clear before writing, because the writing is where much of the clarity gets made. A tool that judges a first draft by finished-text standards is making Magee's mistake.

### 2.3 What an editing tool should NOT flag, and how it could tell the difference

Should not flag, or should flag only softly and label as tertiary:
- **Long sentences, long words, few personal pronouns, nominalisations** when they are the norm of the declared audience or genre. These are philosophy's clarity proxies, not universal ones (ms p.2-3, note 7).
- **Lexical variety.** Not a defect (Russell, Table 1).
- **Technical density that the piece itself supports**: a term used consistently and either defined in the text or standard for the stated readership.
- **Early-draft disorder.** The "brouillon" stage is where ideas form; flagging it as unclear interrupts the inquiry.
- **Sentence-level issues in a paragraph whose point is not yet made.** The paragraph needs a point before it needs polish; Clearpen's point/reason/why-it-matters judge is the right first instrument, and sentence notes should come after it or be suppressed until the paragraph is "point made".

How to tell:
- **Ask for the reader once** (Plan/brief): field, familiarity, what they already accept. Calibrate Insights thresholds (very long sentences, passive, adverbs) to that answer, and show them as "tertiary" with the reader named ("long for a general reader").
- **Baseline against the draft itself**, not an absolute: flag a sentence or passage that is markedly denser than the writer's own surrounding text, not one that is dense in general.
- **Term check instead of jargon check** (code, not model): list terms that recur and are never defined; ask "define, or does your reader already know it?" The writer's answer is recorded and not asked again.
- **Co-occurrence rule**: raise surface density only when it coincides with a structural problem (an undefined term carrying a premise; a long sentence that contains the paragraph's only claim).
- **Let the writer mark a passage as deliberately technical or deliberately difficult**; the tool then stops flagging its surface and keeps checking only its logic and consistency.
- **Order the Review**: presentational (does the argument hold; are the steps there) first, text structure second, sentences last; Hobbs's hierarchy is a ranking rule for notes.

## 3. Menary on writing as thinking

### 3.1 Thesis

Creating and manipulating written sentences is part of the cognitive process itself, not a record of a process that finished in the head; so writing "transforms our cognitive abilities" [paper, abstract]. The frame is *cognitive integration*: a complex mental task is completed "by a co-ordinated interaction between neural processes, bodily processes and manipulating written sentences" [abstract]. The paper has three sections: Harris's criticisms of the classic claims that writing restructured thought (Goody, McLuhan, Ong), as a warning about pitfalls; how integrated cognitive systems work and an application to a "hybrid mental act" in which writing lets us complete complex cognitive tasks; and, following Harris, a "more realistic picture" of how writing restructures thought: writing provides an "autoglottic space" in which a new form of theoretical thinking becomes prevalent, understood through the nature of written vehicles and how we manipulate them [abstract].

### 3.2 Argument steps (deck order, with the paper's structure overlaid)

1. **The manipulation thesis** (Rowlands 1999): cognitive processes are "in part, made up of physical or bodily manipulation of structures in the environment" [deck 2-3]. Two claims: locational (not all in the body) and constitutive (partly constituted by the manipulation).
2. **Cognitive integration**: internal "non-classical" vehicles and processes (neural-network-like) integrate with external "classical" vehicles (written and spoken language, diagrams, mathematical symbols); cognition is hybrid [deck 4, 15]. Explaining it needs both a dynamical account (reciprocal causal interaction) and a normative account (how we learn to manipulate external vehicles according to cognitive norms) [deck 4].
3. **Why external representations matter**: they let us complete tasks that would otherwise be difficult or impossible, and allow manipulations and transformations that could not be done in the head [deck 9]; Peterson's list runs from algebras and diagrams to punctuation systems and tables [deck 10]. Donald: exograms (external memory records) outlast engrams, hold more, travel across media and can be retrieved and manipulated by more means [deck 12]. Sutton: what is written is discrete and stable and does not blend the way biological memory does, and that dissimilarity is exactly what makes it useful [deck 13].
4. **Three classes of manipulation** [deck 8]: biological coupling (animate vision); epistemic actions (using the environment as its own representation, as in rotating Tetris pieces); and *cognitive practices*, the manipulation of external notational systems governed by norms, as in mathematics. Writing is a cognitive practice.
5. **The supracommunicative view of language** (Clark): language facilitates thinking as well as communication [deck 17]. Clark's six ways [deck 19-24]: memory augmentation (notes, diaries); environmental simplification (labels); coordination and reduced on-line deliberation (explicit plans, to-do lists; no need to revisit each step); taming path-dependent learning (generalising beyond the circumstances in which something was learned); attention and resource allocation (externalising control loops: a child reciting times tables; Vygotsky's egocentric speech, illustrated by the four-and-a-half-year-old talking herself through getting candy with a stool and a stick [deck 18]); and **data manipulation and representation**: "writing things down ... allows the ideas to be rearranged and sorted into a new ordering"; brainstorming gets ideas out so they can be compared and contrasted [deck 24].
6. **Problem 1, normativity** [deck 25-28]: a causal-dynamical story of how I move symbols on a page says nothing about why those moves are right or purposeful. Higher cognition needs **cognitive norms**: *purposive* (the activity has an end), *manipulative* (how the notation may be transformed), *corrective* (how to correct manipulations in pursuit of the end), *interpretative* (how to read inscriptions as significant beyond the notation, for the world and others' interests).
7. **Problem 2, Harris and the fixed code** [deck 29-32]: for Harris (integrational linguistics) a sign has no identity outside the context that produced it, so is writing a fixed code? No: but written sentences differ from on-the-fly utterances in that they "can be returned to and manipulated and interpreted in a variety of ways", and those properties (Donald's) are what make them useful. Norms and formats are open-ended, plastic, not fixed laws. Varieties of representation answer varieties of task: to draw inferences from the form; to explore and develop an idea; to transcribe or rearrange music; to build a predictive theory [deck 31].
8. **Autoglottic space** [paper, abstract; background]: Harris's (1989) term for the space writing opens in which language becomes an object for its own scrutiny, detached from speaker and moment: sentences can be inspected, compared, tested for consistency, reordered. This, not literacy as such, is the realistic sense in which writing restructures thought: theoretical thinking becomes prevalent because the vehicles permit it. The integrationist locates the gain in the vehicles and our trained manipulation of them, not in a romantic transformation of the mind.
9. **The hybrid mental act** [abstract; background]: composing an argument on the page. Write a sentence; read it back; see that it does not say what was meant, or that two sentences conflict, or that a step is missing; rewrite, reorder, delete; the "thinking" is the whole loop, including the reading-back and manipulation, not an inner event that the sentences merely report. "Human specialness is ecological" (Don Ross, quoted deck 33).

### 3.3 Implications for a tool that intervenes while someone writes

- **The draft is part of the writer's thinking apparatus, not its output.** A tool that rewrites the writer's sentences on its own initiative is manipulating the writer's cognitive vehicles for them: the manipulation is the thinking, so it is taking the thinking. Clearpen's design (proposals as tracked changes, accepted one by one; never drafting) is the right posture; the principle extends to thinking aids: present *questions, structure and views of the writer's own sentences*, never replacement sentences.
- **Keep the writer's inscription stable.** Written vehicles are useful because they stay put and can be returned to. Anything that changes the text under the writer's hands (silent autocorrect of substance, reflowing structure, inserting text) breaks the property Menary says writing adds. Typo correction is at the margin of this; corrections of wording are not.
- **Timing: intervene at read-back moments.** The loop has a production phase (writing a sentence) and an interpretation phase (reading it back). Intervention belongs in the second: after a finished sentence and a pause, at paragraph or section end, or on request. Mid-sentence or mid-burst intervention interrupts the manipulation (see Galbraith on bursts, section 4).
- **Kinds of prompt: support manipulation, don't perform it.** Helpful: lay the paragraph's sentences out as a list; show the claims the writer has made so far, in their words; let the writer drag sentences into a new order; ask "which of these is the claim?"; mark where a step appears to be missing. These are manipulative and corrective aids in Menary's sense. Harmful: writing the missing step; reordering for them; "improving" the argument.
- **The tool as a norm-mirror.** Menary's four norms give a vocabulary for what a thinking aid may legitimately do: *purposive* (ask what the piece is for and whom it is for; hold the writer to it), *manipulative* (offer ways to rearrange the writer's own material), *corrective* (point out where a manipulation went wrong: this does not follow, this term changed meaning), *interpretative* (ask how a reader outside the notation will take it). None of these requires the tool to generate content.
- **Questions over answers.** Vygotsky's egocentric speech and Clark's "attention and resource allocation" suggest self-directed language is a control structure. A margin question ("what makes you sure of this?") joins the writer's self-talk; a margin answer replaces it.
- **Exploit the exogram's advantages.** Writing beats speech in capacity, durability and rearrangeability. Features that make rearranging and comparing easier (claim lists, section cards, side-by-side views of two versions) amplify what the medium already does for thinking; features that generate more text do not.
- **Autoglottic features.** Anything that lets the writer treat their own sentences as objects (extract every sentence that asserts something; show where a term is used; show two sentences that may conflict side by side) is the tool-version of Harris's space, and is where "theoretical thinking" gets its purchase.

## 4. Cognitive models of writing (Galbraith 2009)

### 4.1 The models

**Hayes & Flower (1980); Flower & Hayes (1980a, b); Hayes & Flower (1986)** (p.8-9)
- Three processes: **planning** (generating ideas, organising, goal-setting), **translating** (converting conceptual content into linguistic form; nothing to do with languages), **reviewing** (reading, editing).
- They operate on two kinds of information: the **task environment** (the assignment and the text produced so far) and **long-term memory** (topic knowledge, a model of the audience, the writing plan, grammar, knowledge of text standards).
- **Recursive, not stages**: any process can occur at any moment; "they refer to cognitive processes rather than stages in the writing process" (p.8). The **monitor** coordinates them: decides when enough content has been generated, when revision is needed, and so on; individual differences are different configurations of production rules for the writing process.
- Expert vs novice: experts build a more elaborate representation of their goals and keep modifying it while writing; they set **explicit rhetorical goals for the whole text** and use them to guide retrieval; novices work from concrete content goals and generate content in response to the topic alone. Experts revise in terms of the text's *function* relative to goals, not just its expression, and change content more during both writing and revision (Hayes et al. 1987).

**Bereiter & Scardamalia (1987): knowledge telling vs knowledge transforming** (p.9-11)
- **Knowledge telling**: text production guided by direct retrieval of content from long-term memory, "organised solely by the associative relationships between content as it is stored" (p.9); topic and genre cues produce the next thing to say; what is written reflects what the writer knows, in the order it comes to mind.
- **Knowledge transforming**: the writer elaborates a representation of the rhetorical or communicative *problem* and derives goals from it that guide the generation and evaluation of content. Formalised as a **dialectic between a content problem space and a rhetorical problem space**: ideas are represented both as knowledge and in terms of their rhetorical function in the text; content is "formulated as the text develops", as a contribution to "the series of rhetorical acts gradually emerging in the text" (p.10). Transformers plan more before, modify plans more radically during, revise more extensively after; their texts fit the reader, and "in adapting their thought to their communicative goals, such writers also develop their understanding of what they are writing about" (p.9).
- Not an evolution of telling but "a radical change in the way that the writing task is defined by the writer" (p.10). The telling model survives inside it as the mechanism of retrieval.
- The goals come from **discourse knowledge** (genre); writing in an unfamiliar genre can mean learning a different way of thinking, even for skilled writers (p.11).

**Cognitive overload** (p.11-13)
- Working memory is limited; translating ideas into well-formed text can consume resources needed for planning. Bourdin & Fayol: children recall fewer items when writing than speaking; even adults do worse in writing when the composition task is complex (2002). So lower components should run as automatically as possible, and strategies that cut load free planning.
- **Kellogg (1988)**: an **outline strategy** (generate and organise before writing, then attend to translation and revision) vs **rough drafting** (translate without worrying about expression; monitor later). Outlining redistributed processing (less planning during production) and gave higher-quality final drafts; rough drafting gave no quality benefit even though revision was postponed. **Kellogg (1990)**: a hierarchically organised outline beats an ordered list, which beats clustering. **Kellogg (1994)**: outlining works because ideas get organised first and resources are then free for formulating them.
- Caveat (Baaijen et al. 2008): dyslexic writers wrote better the more they generated content *while* formulating; robust findings do not generalise to every population. A tool should not force one strategy.

**Hayes (1996)** (p.14)
- Planning becomes part of a general **reflection** component; translating becomes **text production** (a less passive role); revision is not a separate process but **text interpretation + reflection + text production**; working memory is explicit, in Baddeley's multicomponent form.

**Kellogg (1996, 2001): working memory in writing** (p.14-15, Table 1)
- Baddeley's components: central executive (retrieval, attention, supervision), phonological loop (verbal material), visuospatial sketchpad (VSSP).
- Planning: VSSP + central executive (prelinguistic ideas, no verbal component). Translating: central executive + phonological loop. Transcription: programming needs the executive, executing hardly anything for practised writers. Reading one's text: executive + phonological loop. Editing: VSSP (an image of where text is on the page).
- Galbraith et al. (2005, 2009): the **spatial** component is crucial to developing new ideas (knowledge transforming) during outlining. Outlining is a spatial activity.

**Chenoweth & Hayes (2001, 2003); Hayes (2009): text production** (p.15-17, Figure 2)
- Four components: **proposer** (creates an "idea package"; for longer texts also goal-setting and planning) -> **translator** (produces a language string) -> **evaluator/reviser** (accepts it, or sends it back for revision of the string or the idea; "also able to interrupt all other processes at any time") -> **transcriber**.
- Language comes in **bursts** of sentence parts (Kaufer et al. 1986): **P-bursts** end in a pause followed by more production; **R-bursts** are followed by revision. P-burst length depends on the translator's capacity, i.e. the writer's linguistic resources; shorter in L2, with more R-bursts.
- "Ideas are often fleetingly generated at the point of text production and have to be maintained in working memory until the complete sentence has been transcribed" (p.17); burst size affects the complexity of ideas a writer can express and the local coherence of the text.

**Galbraith (1999, 2009a, b): the dual-process model** (p.17-20)
- **Knowledge retrieval** (explicit ideas from long-term memory, either told directly or transformed under rhetorical goals) can only reorganise or reselect existing knowledge.
- **Knowledge constituting**: during spontaneous text production, content is synthesised by constraint satisfaction within implicit semantic memory; because the units are subsymbolic, "the meaning of what the writer wants to say only becomes apparent after the content has been formulated" (p.18). Ideas during production are "temporary patterns of activation", not retrieved objects. Inhibitory feedback from each utterance makes the next utterance express the "remainder", so thought is "self-moving"; to capture it "the writer has to allow the process to unfold without interruption by explicit planning" (p.18).
- Evidence: writers develop ideas more when planning in note form than when producing full text and planning at once (as transforming predicts), *and* they also produce new ideas in spontaneous full-text drafts, associated with growth of personal understanding (p.17).
- The two processes conflict (explicit organisation for the reader vs implicit organisation of one's own understanding) and the balance is tied to the self: presenting a coherent self-image to the reader vs actualising a latent understanding (p.19). Motivation and process feed each other.
- Closing line of the review: "Writing is thinking" (p.20).

### 4.3 Nudging from telling to transforming without writing for the writer

Telling is organised by association in the writer's memory; transforming is organised by the reader's problem. The lever is therefore **making the rhetorical problem space visible and keeping it next to the content**, so that the writer, not the tool, does the dialectic.

- **Build the rhetorical space from questions**: who reads this; what do they believe now; what should they believe after; what will they say against it; why does it matter to them. Store the answers; show them beside the draft; feed them to the judges as context. (Flower & Hayes: experts set explicit rhetorical goals for the whole text; B&S: goals derived from the rhetorical problem guide generation and evaluation.)
- **Ask for function, not content**. Clearpen's point/reason/why-it-matters check is already a rhetorical-space check at paragraph level; extend it with "whose objection does this answer?" and "what must the reader already accept for this to land?". The tool names the missing function and leaves the slot empty.
- **Show content space and rhetorical space side by side**: the writer's notes (what I know) on one side, the outline with the reader's path (what they need, in order) on the other; unplaced notes and unfilled slots are the agenda. The writer moves things across.
- **Surface association**: when a paragraph's sentences are linked only by "and also", say so as a question: "these three points are about the topic; what does the reader learn from their order?"
- **Revision framed as function**: "which paragraph carries the claim?" "the reason appears before the claim it supports; intended?" (Hayes et al. 1987: experts evaluate against function.)
- **Hierarchical, not flat**: give the outline levels (Kellogg 1990), because the hierarchy is where the reader's path and the content's dependency get reconciled.
- **Never the transformed content.** The thesis sentence, the ordering, the objection and its rebuttal are precisely the knowledge the writer develops by transforming; if the tool supplies them, the writer is back to telling, now telling the tool's knowledge.

## 5. Principles

1. **Rank notes by level: presentational, then structural, then sentence-level.** Reason: Hobbs/Magee hierarchy; sentence polish is tertiary and clarity of ideas can exist without it. Apply: Review orders and labels its cards (argument > structure > sentence); sentence notes are suppressed or folded while the paragraph is not "point made"; the LLM review prompt is told to report argument gaps before wording.
2. **Calibrate "clarity" to a declared reader; never apply one community's surface norms universally.** Reason: Hobbs on discourse communities; nominalisation is precision in one field and fog in another. Apply: the Plan brief asks for reader and genre once; Insights thresholds (long sentence, passive, adverbs) are set per genre in code and shown with the reader named; the LLM prompt carries the reader description and is told not to flag conventions of that readership.
3. **Treat lexical variety and long words as neutral unless a term is undefined for the reader.** Reason: Hobbs Table 1 (Russell's STTR). Apply: no STTR or word-length "clarity" metric; a code check lists recurring undefined terms and asks whether the reader knows them; the writer's answer persists.
4. **The draft is the writer's thinking apparatus: never change it unasked, and keep every change visible and reversible.** Reason: Menary's manipulation thesis and exogram stability; Hobbs's Beethovians use the page as an extension of mind. Apply: already Clearpen's tracked edits and versions; extend to thinking aids: aids produce views, lists and questions, never insertions; autocorrect limited to typos with ⌘Z.
5. **Intervene at read-back moments, never inside a burst.** Reason: Chenoweth & Hayes bursts; Galbraith's idea package held in working memory until the sentence is down; Menary's loop of write then read. Apply: triggers are "finished sentence + pause", paragraph end, section end, or an explicit key; no flags appear while typing; next-sentence waits for the pause and never fires twice in a row (already the rule).
6. **One intervention at a time, and the writer's own words between interventions.** Reason: working-memory competition (Kellogg); the reviser in Chenoweth & Hayes can interrupt everything, and a tool is an external reviser. Apply: a global "one AI thing on screen" rule across ghost text, edits and notes; a note waits until the previous one is dismissed or acted on.
7. **Ask for the function of a paragraph before judging its expression.** Reason: experts revise against function (Hayes et al. 1987); B&S rhetorical space. Apply: the point/reason/why-it-matters judge runs before any style pass; Review's LLM prompt uses a fixed function vocabulary (claim, reason, example, qualification, objection, consequence) and reports the missing one.
8. **Make the rhetorical problem explicit and keep it next to the content.** Reason: the telling-to-transforming shift is driven by goals derived from the reader's problem. Apply: a brief with reader, change, stakes, objection; shown as a side panel; injected into every judge prompt; "unplaced notes / unfilled slots" view.
9. **Prefer hierarchical outlines over lists over clusters, built from the writer's material.** Reason: Kellogg 1990; spatial working memory supports new ideas during outlining (Galbraith et al.). Apply: the outline tool nests; it can be seeded from the draft's headings and the writer's notes; it never generates headings the writer has not written, though it may ask "what goes here?".
10. **Offer strategies, don't impose one.** Reason: outlining helps most writers but not all (Baaijen et al. 2008); Mozartians and Beethovians both exist (Hobbs). Apply: Plan is optional; free-write mode is optional; the empty-article hint keeps both "Plan this article" and "Just write".
11. **Protect spontaneous drafting: a mode with all AI off, followed by a discovery pass.** Reason: Galbraith's knowledge-constituting process requires the text to unfold "without interruption by explicit planning"; new ideas appear in spontaneous drafts. Apply: a toggle that silences next-sentence, autocorrect and notes for a session; afterwards a code+LLM pass lists sentences that assert something not in the outline or brief and asks "keep, move, or drop?".
12. **Questions, not answers; prompts, not prose.** Reason: Menary's cognitive norms (corrective and interpretative) can be embodied by questions; Vygotsky's self-directed speech is a control loop that a question joins and an answer replaces; Clearpen's own principle. Apply: thinking-aid prompts are instructed to output a single question or a labelled gap, never a sentence for the draft; a validator in code rejects outputs that look like draft prose (declarative, in the article's voice) and re-asks.
13. **Treat the writer's sentences as objects: extract, list, compare, reorder.** Reason: Harris's autoglottic space via Menary; Clark's data manipulation; exograms are rearrangeable. Apply: "claims in this section" list in the writer's exact words; two possibly conflicting sentences shown side by side (Clearpen's contradiction check already finds them); draggable sentence or paragraph cards.
14. **Bring the reader in early, as a voice that asks.** Reason: Hobbs on the reader's direct involvement in forming ideas; B&S rhetorical goals. Apply: an "objection" aid phrased as a reader's question at section end; Ask's starter questions are reader questions ("why should I believe the second paragraph?"), not tasks ("summarise").
15. **Arithmetic and structure checks in code; judgement in the model; both say where they came from.** Reason: Hobbs's measurable proxies are cheap and honest when labelled; model judgements are fallible. Apply: already Clearpen's rule; extend to outline coverage, undefined terms and function tags where a deterministic check is possible.
16. **Do not judge an early draft by finished-text standards.** Reason: Hobbs's brouillon stage; Galbraith's constituting process. Apply: Review on a short or freshly started draft defaults to argument-level questions and withholds sentence notes; the writer can ask for the full pass.

## 6. Thinking-aid feature ideas

1. **The brief as five questions (Plan).** Before or during writing, the writer answers: who reads this; what they believe now; what they should believe after; what they will object to; why it matters to them. Shown in a side panel, saved with the article, fed into every judge. Justification: Flower & Hayes's explicit rhetorical goals; Bereiter & Scardamalia's rhetorical problem space. The tool asks; the writer writes.

2. **Argument steps, in the writer's words.** On request (or at section end), the section's claim-bearing sentences are listed as a numbered ladder, verbatim, with a "?" row where a step appears to be missing or where a reason precedes its claim. Nothing is written into the gap; the writer fills it or dismisses it. Justification: Menary's autoglottic space (sentences as objects to inspect); Hobbs's presentational clarity; the Hayes monitor.

3. **Paragraph function tags in the margin.** Each paragraph gets a quiet label from a fixed vocabulary (claim, reason, example, qualification, objection, consequence, transition) and an empty slot for the function the paragraph seems to need next. Replaces nothing; extends the existing point/reason/why-it-matters check to the whole draft. Justification: experts revise against function (Hayes et al. 1987); B&S.

4. **The reader's one question (counter).** After a section, a single question in a sceptical reader's voice ("what would convince someone who thinks X?"), built from the brief's objection and the section's claims; never an answer, never a rebuttal paragraph. Justification: Hobbs on the reader's direct involvement in forming ideas; B&S rhetorical goals; Clearpen's principle. This is how the plan's "counter" aid stays a thinking aid.

5. **Free-write, then discover.** A mode that turns off ghost text, autocorrect and notes for a timed stretch; afterwards a pass lists sentences that assert something not in the brief or outline and asks "keep, move, or drop?", and offers to add kept ones to the outline. Justification: Galbraith's knowledge-constituting process (unfold without interruption; meaning appears after formulation); versions make it safe.

6. **Outline against draft.** A two-column view: the hierarchical outline (nested, draggable, built from the writer's headings and notes) and the draft's paragraphs mapped onto it; outline nodes with no paragraph and paragraphs with no node are shown as the open agenda. Justification: Kellogg 1990 (hierarchy beats list); Hayes & Flower monitor (is there enough content?); Galbraith et al. on spatial working memory.

7. **Sentence cards for a section.** Turn a selected section into cards, one per sentence or paragraph, to drag into a new order; "Apply order" writes the writer's own sentences back in the new sequence as a tracked change. Justification: Clark's "data manipulation and representation" via Menary; Hobbs's Beethovians moving ideas around the page; exograms are rearrangeable.

8. **Terms this reader may not know.** A code check lists terms that recur and are never defined, with the brief's reader named ("used 6 times; defined nowhere; your reader is a general one"); the writer answers "they know it" or "define", and the answer sticks. Justification: Hobbs on discourse-community relativity and on jargon as the one surface feature with a structural cost (Joll's explication of terms).

A note on the plan's "opening" and "ending" aids: on these texts, an opening or ending *written* by the tool is knowledge telling in the tool's voice. The same aids survive as questions: "what does the reader need to care about in the first paragraph?", "what should they do or believe when they finish?".

## 8. Assessment: the highest-value ideas

1. **Function before expression, and a ranked Review.** All three texts converge: Hobbs ranks sentence clarity last; Bereiter & Scardamalia and Hayes et al. locate expertise in evaluating function; Galbraith warns against judging a forming draft by finished standards. Clearpen's point/reason/why-it-matters judge is already the right instrument; making it the gate for style notes, and ordering Review cards by level, is cheap and changes the app's whole stance from proofreader to editor.

2. **The brief as the rhetorical problem space, fed to every judge.** This is the single lever for the telling-to-transforming shift, and it also solves Hobbs's calibration problem (what counts as clear for *this* reader). Five stored answers make the next-sentence judge, Review and the counter aid reader-specific without any generated prose.

3. **Read-back timing and the one-thing-on-screen rule.** The burst model and working-memory competition say exactly when help is harm: inside a sentence, or when something else is already on screen. Clearpen's existing "pause after a finished sentence; never twice in a row" is the correct rule; it should be made global across all AI surfaces, and stated in the LLM instructions as a reason, not just a setting.

4. **Autoglottic views: claims list, argument steps, sentence cards.** Menary gives the deepest justification for a thinking aid that never writes: writing helps thinking because sentences become stable objects one can inspect and rearrange. Views that extract and let the writer move their own sentences amplify precisely that, and they are mostly code (extraction, drag-and-drop, tracked reordering) with a small model role (which sentences carry claims).

5. **Free-write mode with a discovery pass.** Galbraith's dual-process model is the one piece of evidence here that *less* tool is sometimes the feature: spontaneous drafting generates ideas that planning does not, if left alone. A silence toggle plus a "what did you discover?" list respects both halves of the model and gives Clearpen a principled answer to "why is the AI quiet?".
