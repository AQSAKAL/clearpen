## 1. Source

### What it is
- Anne Eisenberg (b. 1942), then at Polytechnic University, Brooklyn (humanities faculty; author of several McGraw-Hill/Harper technical-writing textbooks and later a *New York Times* technology columnist).
- *Guide to Technical Editing: Discussion, Dictionary, and Exercises.* New York: Oxford University Press, 9 April 1992. Paperback, ix + 182 pp. (OUP/Amazon say 192 with front matter), 49 illustrations. ISBN 0-19-506306-6 / 978-0-19-506306-6. LCCN 91-16696. LC class T11.4 .E38 1992; Dewey 808/.0666. LC subject: *Technical editing*. Includes index. OCLC 23766691 (and 26310446, 299740903, 781041826, 877752872).
- The subtitle is literal: the book has three parts, a **Discussion** (chapters of before-and-after editing with marginal Problem/Solution notes), a **Dictionary** (pp. 85–125, a technical editor's glossary that doubles as a stylebook), and **Exercises** (eight long multi-error editing exercises at the end). A short Notes section and an index close it.
- Audience, in the author's own framing (quoted by a reader): students in technical-writing courses who want editing; technical professionals who must edit their own work or the work of people reporting to them; and people from countries whose technical-editing conventions differ from US ones. The publisher adds "working scientists, engineers" and non-native speakers of English.

### 2a. The book's own organisation [E] + [I]

The method, as far as it can be seen: **show the flawed passage, name the problem in the margin, show the edited passage, and where possible show more than one acceptable edit**. Akinci stresses that Eisenberg routinely gives alternative edits, which he reads as respecting the writer's freedom. The publisher stresses editing "for accuracy, clarity, and grammar" and "from the reader's point of view", and singles out editing **abstracts** as a core exercise type.

What the book does *not* appear to contain (and nobody claims it does): a formal five-level JPL scheme, production management, or the legal/ethical chapters of Rude. It is a workbook on the **language, substantive and mechanical-style edits of short technical prose, titles, abstracts, tables and figures**, with a stylebook attached.

### 2b. The field's framework she works within [F]

**Van Buren & Buehler (JPL, 1976/1980).** Nine *types* of edit, combined cumulatively into five *levels*. Table 1 of the booklet:

| Type | L1 | L2 | L3 | L4 | L5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coordination (job handling, author liaison, author checks) | x | x | x | x | x |
| Policy (required elements present; no endorsements or derogatory remarks; SI units) | x | x | x | x | x |
| Integrity (parts of the document agree with each other) | x | x | x | x | |
| Screening (minimum acceptable text: spelling, S–V agreement, complete sentences, incomprehensible statements clarified) | x | x | x | x | |
| Copy Clarification (make copy producible) | x | x | x | | |
| Format (typography, layout) | x | x | x | | |
| Mechanical Style (consistency: capitalisation, spelling variants, compounding, numerals, abbreviations/units, citation order, spacing around operators, nomenclature) | x | x | | | |
| Language (how ideas are expressed) | x | x | | | |
| Substantive (meaningful content) | x | | | | |

Two points in the booklet matter for Clearpen more than the table:
1. **Types can be used independently** of levels when there is no institutional minimum — e.g., an author may ask for "an Integrity Edit and a Language Edit and nothing more". That is exactly a menu of editing tasks the author picks from.
2. **Every change in a Language Edit is made for a specific, identifiable reason, never editor preference.** The booklet also warns against both the heavy-handed editor who excises rhetorical effectiveness while hunting wordiness and the timid editor who lets rhetoric obscure meaning.

The Language Edit checklist (12 items): spelling to the dictionary; grammar and syntax; punctuation to the style manual; usage (Follett); fluency including transitions; **language parallelism** (in lists, headings, outline structure, grammatical constructions); conciseness; proper use of description/exposition/narrative/argument and their effect on tense; inconsistent or erroneous terminology; definition of abbreviations, acronyms, symbols; completeness of fences in maths; appropriateness of slide titles.

The Substantive Edit checklist (14 items for the whole document, then tables, figures, slides): title reflects content, concise; abstract concise and informative enough to judge content; sections contain what their headings promise; appendix material belongs there; material grouped and subdivided rationally; parallel ideas given equal weight, subordination logical; emphasis proportional to significance and reflected in heading structure; repetition and redundancy removed; **discrepancies in meaning between parts resolved** (statements that contradict; table/figure content differing from the text); **missing material identified and brought to the author's attention**; irrelevant material recommended for deletion to the author; need for a symbols/abbreviations list flagged; need for additional references flagged; permissions flagged. Tables: units in column heads not data cells; variables and units identified in boxhead/stub; parallel form across similar tables; powers of ten unambiguous; columns ordered for comprehension. Figures: all curves/axes identified; excess detail removed; scales on photos.

The Integrity Edit checklist (11 items): contents list agrees with headings and captions; page numbers agree; every table/figure/reference/footnote/appendix is cited and every citation points at something that exists; no duplicate or missing numbers in any sequence; sequences consistent in kind; no two captions identical; references to other text elements exist; series volumes consistent.

**Rude, *Technical Editing* (1991–2010).** Two levels for teaching: *copyediting* (correctness and consistency; grammar, punctuation, mechanics, format) and *comprehensive editing* (content, organisation, visual design, style, usability). Process: analyse readers/purpose/uses → evaluate the document → set objectives → plan → review the plan with the author → edit, **deepest level first so surface work isn't wasted on text that will change** → evaluate.

**Tarutz (1992).** Six informal depths: turning pages; skimming (obvious spelling/grammar/punctuation); skimming and comparing (internal consistency, cross-references); reading (wording and usage); analysing (organisation, missing information, redundancy, technical inconsistency); testing and using.

### 2c. The procedure a tool could follow (synthesis) [F]+[I]

Order the passes from deepest to most superficial, and let the author pick the depth.

1. **Scope check (Substantive, document level).** Does the title say what the piece is about, concisely? Does the lede/abstract state the result, not the topic? Does each section contain what its heading promises? Is anything missing that the piece promised (gaps), out of order (transpositions), or said twice (redundancies)? Are parallel ideas given parallel weight in the heading structure? *Output: notes to the author; never supply the missing content.*
2. **Consistency check (Substantive §9 + Integrity).** Do statements in different places contradict each other? Do numbers in prose match numbers in tables? Do totals add up? Does every figure/table have a caption and a mention in the text, and does every mention point at something that exists? Are numbering sequences complete and unique? Is terminology consistent (one name per thing)? *Output: notes; mostly code.*
3. **Language pass (Language Edit), paragraph by paragraph.** Density; conciseness; parallelism in lists and headings; transitions; tense consistent with mode; abbreviations defined at first use; vague or sweeping statements. *Output: tracked, pinpoint edits with a stated reason, more than one option where reasonable.*
4. **Screening pass (minimum correctness).** Spelling; subject–verb agreement; complete sentences; sentences that cannot be understood flagged (not rewritten). *Output: tracked edits.*
5. **Mechanical-style pass.** Capitalisation; spelling variants; compounding/hyphenation of compound modifiers; words vs digits for numbers; units and abbreviations; citation order; spacing around operators; heading case. *Output: tracked edits, or a style-sheet of inconsistencies for the author to choose a convention.*
6. **Captions, titles, tables, figures (Eisenberg's own emphasis).** Caption is informative, not a bare label; table units in heads; stub and boxhead complete; axes labelled.

The author chooses which passes run (Clearpen's menu already maps onto this: *Fix grammar* = 4+5; *Shorten/Simplify* = 3; *Review* = 1+2; *Improve* = 3 with a touch of 1).

## 3. Principles

Each: (a) rule, (b) reason, (c) example where one exists, (d) detection. Source tags as above.

1. **Lighten density.** [E] (a) The characteristic fault of technical writers is packing too much into each clause; edit to spread it out. (b) Readers who are not the author cannot unpack it. (c) The book's opening sentence states this as its premise. (d) Code: mean words per sentence, clause depth, prepositional-phrase chains ("of the … of the …"), noun strings ≥3, nominalisation ratio (-tion/-ment/-ance nouns per verb). Model: propose a lighter version, offering two alternatives.

2. **Accuracy before clarity before grammar.** [E]+[F] (a) Check that it is right, then that it is clear, then that it is correct. (b) Polishing a sentence that will be changed or cut is wasted work, and polish can hide error. (c) Publisher: edits "for accuracy, clarity, and grammar"; Rude: deepest level first. (d) Procedure, not detector: run substantive/consistency passes before language/mechanics, and suppress mechanical suggestions on sentences already carrying a substantive note.

3. **Edit from the reader's point of view.** [E] (a) Ask what a reader who was not there needs first, and reorder/cut to that. (b) Authors recount their work in the order they did it; readers want the result and its reason. (c) Publisher: exercises in "presenting technical information from the reader's point of view". (d) Model: paragraph-level judgement "what does the reader learn first vs. what should they learn first". Code pre-filter: paragraph opens with narrative past ("We began by…", "First we…").

4. **Abstracts and ledes must be informative, not descriptive.** [E]+[F] (a) An abstract (or lede, summary, caption) states findings and numbers, not the topics covered. (b) The reader decides from it whether to read on. (c) Glossary entry "informative abstract"; JPL Substantive §2: abstract gives enough to judge the general content. (d) Code: first paragraph contains no number and no result-verb (found/shows/reduced/increased) while the body does; "this article discusses/describes" openers. Model: judge whether the opening states a claim.

5. **Every figure and table must be explained.** [E] (a) A graphic needs an informative caption and a sentence in the text saying what to see in it. (b) Readers skip unlabelled figures or misread them. (c) Sidebar on p. 60: "Problem: Detailed figure numbered, but not explained"; the solution is an informative caption naming the thing shown. (d) Code: image/table with no caption or a caption that is only "Figure n"/"Table n"; figure never referred to in prose. Model: does the caption say what the reader should notice?

6. **Titles say precisely what the piece is about, concisely.** [E]+[F] (a) Edit titles for content and length. (b) The title is read by far more people than the body. (c) Eisenberg has a chapter on editing titles; JPL Substantive §1. (d) Code: title is a single noun or a bare topic ("Results", "Overview"); title length > 15 words. Model: does the title match the body's claim?

7. **Headings and sections keep their promises.** [F] (a) Each section contains the material its heading announces, and the heading hierarchy mirrors the subordination of ideas. (b) Readers navigate by headings. (d) Code: heading-level skips (H1→H3), lone subheading (an "a" with no "b" — JPL calls this nonparallel structure), headings that are not parallel in form. Model: section body vs heading mismatch.

8. **Offer alternatives; preserve the writer's choice.** [E] (a) When more than one edit is acceptable, show more than one. (b) Editing is not a single correct answer; the author owns the voice. (c) Akinci: Eisenberg gives "more than one editing option". (d) Not a detector: a generation rule for Improve/Shorten/Simplify (produce two variants when the change is stylistic rather than corrective).

9. **Name the problem before showing the fix.** [E] (a) Every change is accompanied by a one-line statement of what was wrong. (b) The author learns and can disagree; unexplained changes get rejected or resented. (c) The Problem/Solution sidebars keyed to each before/after. (d) Generation rule: every tracked edit carries a problem label from a fixed taxonomy (density, nonparallel list, undefined abbreviation, unsupported claim, …) plus a short reason.

10. **Changes for identifiable reasons, not preference.** [F] (a) An editor does not change what is already acceptable. (b) Avoid churn and voice-flattening. (c) JPL: Language Edit changes rest on "specific and identifiable reasons". (d) Model instruction: if the sentence has no nameable fault, leave it; code: cap the edit ratio per paragraph and surface "no changes needed" as a positive result.

11. **Balance: neither heavy-handed nor timid.** [F] (a) Cut wordiness without excising emphasis or rhetoric that carries meaning; but do not let rhetoric obscure meaning. (c) JPL's two cautionary editors. (d) Model instruction only; code guard: reject edits that remove hedges or qualifiers attached to numbers or claims (see 20).

12. **Parallelism in lists, headings and series.** [F] (a) Items in a list or heading series share grammatical form; outline structure is balanced (no "(i)" without "(ii)"). (b) Nonparallel form makes the reader re-parse. (c) JPL's example of two headings, one "The advantages of X", the other "X disadvantages". (d) Code: POS/first-token pattern of list items (all imperatives vs. mixed; noun phrases vs. clauses); heading casing/form; lone sub-items. Model: confirm and propose the parallel form.

13. **Conciseness.** [F]+[E] (a) Remove words that carry no information. (d) Code: known filler patterns (in order to, the fact that, it is … that, there is/are openers, due to the fact, at this point in time, redundant pairs), passive with "by" agents, doubled intensifiers. Model: Shorten with reasons.

14. **Define abbreviations, acronyms and symbols at first use; drop ones used once.** [F] (a) Expand on first use; don't abbreviate a term used only once. (d) Code: regex for ALL-CAPS tokens 2–6 letters not preceded by an expansion in parentheses; count of uses.

15. **One term per thing (consistent terminology).** [F] (a) Do not alternate between synonyms for the same referent ("customer/client/user"). (b) Readers assume different words mean different things. (d) Code: candidate synonym sets and spelling variants (disk/disc, e-mail/email, data base/database) with counts; model to confirm referential identity.

16. **Tense follows mode.** [F] (a) Narrative of what was done in past; exposition of what is true in present; descriptions of procedure in imperative/present. (d) Code: tense-shift detector within a paragraph; model to judge whether the shift is a fault.

17. **Transitions and fluency.** [F] (a) Sentences and paragraphs connect; the relation between consecutive statements is marked or evident. (d) Model (Clearpen's "has the paragraph made its point" judge is already this). Code pre-filter: paragraph of ≥3 sentences with no connective and no repeated subject.

18. **Rational grouping; redundancy out.** [F] (a) Material is grouped and subdivided sensibly; nothing is said twice unless for deliberate emphasis. (d) Code: near-duplicate sentence detection (high token overlap across paragraphs); model: is the repeat intentional?

19. **No contradictions between parts; prose matches tables.** [F] (a) Statements in different places agree; numbers in text equal numbers in tables; totals add. (c) JPL Substantive §9 names both. (d) Code: Clearpen already does arithmetic and contradiction checks; extend with "number in prose vs same quantity in table" matching by label. Model: semantic contradiction between distant sentences.

20. **Sweeping and unsupported claims are queried, not softened silently.** [F]+[I] (a) Universal quantifiers and strong causal claims without support get a note asking for evidence or a qualifier; the editor does not invent the qualifier. (c) JPL Substantive §13: need for additional references brought to the author's attention. (d) Code: all/every/never/always/no one/proves; model: claim needs support (Clearpen does both already).

21. **Missing material is flagged, never supplied.** [F] (a) When facts, steps, definitions or a required element are missing, tell the author what is missing and where. (b) Only the author knows the content. (c) JPL Substantive §10, §12. (d) Model: "this paragraph promises X and never gives it"; code: list introduced by "three reasons:" with two items; "as shown below" with nothing below; "see Table 2" with no Table 2.

22. **Irrelevant material is recommended for deletion, not deleted.** [F] (a) Suggest cutting what does not serve the reader (JPL's example is a flowery tribute to a contractor). (d) Model note; the author decides.

23. **Integrity: cross-references, numbering, captions.** [F] (a) Everything cited exists; everything that exists is cited; numbering has no gaps or duplicates; no two captions identical; TOC matches headings. (d) Entirely code.

24. **Screening minimum: spelling, agreement, complete sentences, comprehensibility.** [F] (a) The least an editor does. (d) Code for the first three (Clearpen's autocorrect + Fix grammar); model for "incomprehensible statement, probably missing words".

25. **Mechanical style is about consistency, decided once.** [F]+[E] (a) Capitalisation of content words (Earth/earth), spelling variants, compound modifiers (solid-state as modifier vs solid state as noun), words vs digits, unit abbreviations, serial comma, heading case: pick a convention and apply it everywhere. (c) Glossary SIPs: *compound modifiers*, *use initial capitals*; JPL Mechanical Style list. (d) Code: build a style sheet of the document's own majority choices and flag minority variants (JPL: "standardize on the choices used in the majority of instances" when no guide is given).

26. **Tables: units in heads, complete stub and boxhead, parallel form.** [F]+[E] (a) Units belong in column headings not cells; every column and row labelled; similar tables laid out the same way; powers of ten unambiguous. (c) Glossary SIP *stub column*; JPL table checklist. (d) Code on Markdown tables: units repeated in every cell of a column; empty header cells; sibling tables with different column sets.

27. **Marks, not retyping.** [E]+[F] (a) The editor marks the author's copy with standard copy-editing/proofreading marks and queries; the author keeps the text. (c) Dictionary SIPs *copy editing marks*, *proofreading marks*; JPL "author checks of edited manuscripts". (d) Product rule: tracked, pinpoint changes with accept/reject (Clearpen already) plus marginal queries for anything that is not a one-for-one substitution.

28. **Real documents have many problems at once; triage.** [E] (a) After learning one fault type at a time, the editor must handle passages with several faults and decide which matter. (c) The eight end exercises are multi-error by design. (d) Review ranking: order notes by level (substantive > consistency > language > mechanical) and by position; collapse mechanical notes on a sentence that already has a substantive note.

## 4. The usage dictionary

### Kinds of entries [E]
Akinci, who owns the book, lists three kinds across pp. 85–125:
1. **Basic terms in grammar, usage and style** — the stylebook function; the publisher's "stylebook entries".
2. **Printing and production terms** — the vocabulary an editor needs to talk to typesetters and designers.
3. **Definitions of the major types of technical writing** — document genres.

The publisher calls it "a technical editor's glossary with stylebook entries as well as common terms" and names **informative abstract** and **callout** as examples. The entries are illustrated (the "rocket" on p. 88 is inside the dictionary range, probably illustrating *callout*, *exploded view* or *cutaway*).

### Entries actually recovered [E]
From the publisher copy and Amazon's full-text key phrases (phrases rare elsewhere but frequent here are almost certainly headwords or their definitions):
- informative abstract (and by implication descriptive abstract)
- callout
- stub column (table anatomy; implies boxhead, column head, stub, rules)
- compound modifiers (hyphenation of unit modifiers)

### Entries almost certainly present, inferred from the three categories and the JPL/Rude canon [I]
Grammar/usage/style: active/passive voice; agreement (subject–verb, pronoun); antecedent; dangling/misplaced modifier; parallelism; nominalisation; noun string; comma splice; run-on; fragment; restrictive vs nonrestrictive clause (that/which); hyphen vs en dash vs em dash; numbers (words vs figures); units and SI; abbreviations and acronyms; capitalisation; serial comma; tense; jargon; wordiness/redundancy; sexist language (a 1992 stylebook staple); confusables (affect/effect, principal/principle, fewer/less, data is/are, comprise/compose, which/that).
Printing/production: boxhead, stub, rule, leaders, running head, folio, widow and orphan, galley, page proof, camera-ready, halftone, screen, bleed, crop, justified/ragged, leading, point, pica, serif, font/typeface, caption vs legend vs title, callout, exploded view, cutaway, line art, flush left.
Genres: abstract (informative/descriptive), executive summary, proposal, progress report, feasibility report, specification, manual/instructions, memo, laboratory notebook (Eisenberg wrote on this), literature review, technical article/paper, poster, oral presentation/viewgraph.

How Clearpen could use this: the grammar/usage class of entries is the seed for a **usage checker** (confusable pairs, compound-modifier hyphenation, number style, abbreviation expansion, capitalisation consistency); the genre class is a seed for **genre-aware review** (an "abstract/lede" that is descriptive gets the informative-abstract note); the production class is mostly irrelevant to a Markdown article except *caption*, *callout*, *table anatomy*.

## 5. Editor–author relationship

What can be said with evidence:

- **Explain, then change.** [E] The book's page design is the relationship: beside every before/after sits a *Problem* note and a *Solution* note. The author sees the diagnosis, not just the result. This is the single clearest lesson of the book for Clearpen: a tracked change without a stated problem is a half-edit.
- **More than one right answer.** [E] Eisenberg shows alternative edits and Akinci reads this as protecting the writer's "creative freedom". An editing tool that produces one canonical rewrite overclaims.
- **The editor marks; the author's text stays the author's.** [E]+[F] Copy-editing and proofreading marks are dictionary entries; JPL's Coordination Edit includes author checks of edited manuscripts; the Language Edit at JPL is returned to the author who arranges retyping. Nothing is rewritten wholesale.
- **Missing, irrelevant or unsupported content is *brought to the author's attention*.** [F] JPL uses that phrase repeatedly (Substantive §10–§14): identified, flagged, recommended, never supplied or silently removed. The editor assists with a symbols list only "if necessary" and at the author's request.
- **Agree the depth of edit first.** [F] The whole point of levels of edit is that the author knows in advance what will and will not be touched, can ask for types independently, and can trade depth against time. Clearpen's menu (Fix grammar / Shorten / Simplify / Improve / custom) is a levels-of-edit contract in miniature; the Review should likewise say which passes it ran.
- **Reasons, not taste.** [F] Every Language-Edit change is justified by a specific, identifiable reason; the booklet explicitly rules out the editor's personal preferences.
- **Tone of queries.** [F, Open TC after Mackiewicz & Riley 2003] Write a comment rather than make the change unless the change is simple or pre-authorised; state an opinion when a change is wanted; use should/will/ought for suggestions, softened by "probably" where appropriate; use can/could for optional changes; ask a question only when you genuinely do not know the answer; avoid bare hints and passive modals ("it might be considered…"). Keep an alphabetised style sheet of recurring decisions so the author sees a consistent rule, not a series of whims.
- **Who the editor is.** [E] Eisenberg's audience includes authors editing their own work and managers editing subordinates' reports — i.e. she writes for people who are *both* author and editor, which is Clearpen's user exactly.

## 6. Assessment for an app that edits but never writes

Confidence: **high** on the Levels-of-Edit checklists (primary text read in full); **moderate** on the book's structure and emphases (publisher copy, full-text key phrases, one detailed first-hand description, the scan's section map all agree); **low** on any specific wording or rule inside the book beyond the few items quoted above. The book is a workbook, not a theory; its value to Clearpen is the *method* (problem → solution, levelled passes, marks not rewrites) more than any single rule.

Highest-value ideas, in order:

1. **Problem/Solution labelling on every edit** (code + model). Give each pinpoint change a problem tag from a small fixed taxonomy (density, nonparallel list, undefined abbreviation, vague caption, redundant pair, agreement, tense shift, unsupported claim, inconsistent term/spelling) and a one-line reason. The model produces the edit *and* the tag; code validates the tag against the taxonomy and can veto edits whose tag does not match the diff (e.g., tag "conciseness" but the edit adds words). Fits the "AI proposes, you decide" model exactly and makes rejections informative.

2. **Levelled passes, deepest first, with the user choosing the level** (procedure; mostly code orchestration). Map the menu to JPL types: Review = Substantive + Integrity (notes only); Improve = Language; Shorten/Simplify = Language subsets; Fix grammar = Screening + Mechanical Style. Order Review notes by level and suppress mechanical notes on sentences already flagged substantively (Rude's rule that surface edits on doomed text are wasted). Tell the user which passes ran.

3. **An Integrity/Consistency pass that needs no model** (code). Cross-references that point nowhere; figures/tables never mentioned; captions that are bare labels; numbering gaps/duplicates; heading-level skips and lone sub-items; undefined acronyms; one-off abbreviations; terminology and spelling variants with counts; number-style and capitalisation inconsistencies resolved by the document's own majority convention; units repeated in table cells instead of heads; numbers in prose that disagree with the same quantity in a table. This is the JPL Integrity + Mechanical Style edit, and it is deterministic, fast, and explainable — the kind of note Clearpen already labels "from Clearpen's rules".

4. **Informative-abstract test for ledes, titles and captions** (code pre-filter + model). Eisenberg's own emphasis (abstracts, titles, captions each get a chapter or exercise set). Code finds candidates: a title or caption that is a bare noun or label; an opening paragraph with no claim-verb and no number while the body has them; "this article discusses…" openers. The model asks one question: does this state what the reader will learn, or only what it is about? The existing "has the paragraph made its point" judge is the same muscle aimed at the whole piece's opening.

5. **Density as the headline metric, with alternatives on fix** (code + model). The book's thesis sentence. Code scores sentences for density (length, nominalisations, noun strings, preposition chains) and highlights the worst; the model offers *two* lighter versions with the problem named, honouring both Eisenberg's alternatives and JPL's warning against the heavy hand (never strip qualifiers attached to numbers or claims).

Lower priority but cheap: parallelism in lists and headings (code POS pattern + model fix); tense-shift detector; a style sheet panel that shows the document's own conventions and the outliers.

Not applicable or out of scope: format/typography, copy clarification, policy edits, and the eight teaching exercises (Clearpen edits articles; it does not teach editing — though a "why was this flagged" explanation per note is the teaching the book does in the margin).
